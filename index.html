The Astrophysics Breakdown

Decoding Spacetime Distortion

When observing the black hole, you see a glowing flat ring that appears to curve above, below, and across the central darkness. This isn't a physical cross-shaped ring! It's a flat, circular accretion disk orbiting the equator, but the black hole's intense gravity bends the paths of light emitted behind it.

Try it: Drag the canvas to rotate the camera. Notice how the disk remains locked in the equatorial plane, while the warped image bends dynamically to face you.

Gravitational Lensing

Massive objects warp the very fabric of spacetime. Light rays, which normally travel in straight lines, must follow these curves. The black hole acts as a colossal gravitational lens, magnifying and redirecting light from background structures.

To render this simulation, the app uses backward ray-tracing. Instead of tracing trillions of light rays originating from the accretion disk and hoping they hit the camera, we do the reverse: we shoot invisible "scout" photons from the camera pixel into the curved space.

At each step, we solve the equations of motion derived from the Schwarzschild metric using the 4th-Order Runge-Kutta (RK4) integrator.

Equation of Motion (Binet Approximation)
a = -1.5 · h² · r / r⁵

Where h is the conserved specific angular momentum and r is the distance to the center. This equation pulls the light ray inward, causing it to loop or spiral around the singularity.

Marching Results

  • Hit Horizon: If a ray marches inside $r < r_{s}$, it falls into the black hole and renders black.
  • Hit Disk: If it intersects the disk plane, it samples the gas brightness.
  • Escape: If it flees to infinity, it samples the background starfield, which is itself warped.

The black hole's boundary is the Event Horizon. It is the boundary where gravity is so strong that the escape velocity exceeds the speed of light ($c$). Since nothing can travel faster than light, nothing—not even light—can escape.

Try it: Toggle Naked Singularity. This removes the event horizon, allowing you to peek into the central core where light rays twist into infinitely orbiting Einstein rings.

Doppler Beaming & Shifting

The accretion disk is rotating at relativistic speeds (a significant fraction of light speed). Because of this, light from the gas moving towards you (the left side of the disk, if rotating counter-clockwise) is compressed or blueshifted (hotter, brighter). Conversely, the gas moving away from you (the right side) is stretched or redshifted (cooler, dimmer). This produces the signature brightness asymmetry seen in physical simulations.
